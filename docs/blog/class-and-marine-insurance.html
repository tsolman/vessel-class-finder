Why does marine insurance depend on a ship keeping its class?

Published 19 October 2026 by WeAreFabbrik. Figures from the IACS Vessels in Class dataset as of 30 September 2026.

Insurers rely on class as independent evidence that a ship is built and maintained to recognised technical standards. Hull and machinery policies and P&I club rules therefore typically require the ship to be classed with an approved society and to keep that class. If class is suspended, withdrawn or changed, cover may terminate or be affected, depending on the policy wording.

Why insurers care about class

An underwriter cannot inspect every ship on a book. A classification society does that work. It surveys the hull, machinery and key systems at set intervals, and it can require repairs as a condition of keeping class. Our guide to what a classification society does covers the basics.

For an insurer, class status is a proxy for condition. It does not guarantee a ship is safe, but it shows that a recognised body has surveyed it on schedule and found it acceptable. That is why most mainstream hull and P&I placements typically expect class with a member of IACS, or with a society the insurer has specifically approved.

The classification clause in hull and machinery cover

Hull and machinery policies usually contain a classification clause, sometimes drafted as a warranty or as a termination condition. The exact words vary by market and by policy, but the common elements are:

  • The ship must be classed with a society agreed by the insurer.
  • Class must be maintained throughout the policy period.
  • Recommendations and conditions of class must be complied with by the dates the society sets.
  • The assured must tell insurers about certain changes, for example a change of society.

Widely used London market hull wordings, such as the Institute Time Clauses (Hulls), have long included a clause under which cover can end automatically if the classification society changes, or if class is changed, suspended, discontinued, withdrawn or expires, unless insurers agree otherwise in writing. Such clauses often allow cover to continue until the ship reaches its next port if the event happens at sea. Other markets and bespoke wordings deal with the point differently.

The practical point: check your policy wording. Whether loss of class ends cover, suspends it or only matters if it caused the loss depends on the clause and the governing law. In English law, for example, the Insurance Act 2015 changed how breaches of warranty work, but an express termination clause may still apply. Take advice on the specific policy.

Class and P&I cover

P&I covers third-party liabilities such as crew injury, pollution, wreck removal and cargo claims. Most ocean-going tonnage is entered with one of the clubs in the International Group of P&I Clubs, and their rules typically include conditions on class. These usually require that the ship:

  • is and stays classed with a society approved by the club;
  • complies with the society's rules, recommendations and survey requirements;
  • has its class records made available to the club when asked.

Under many club rules, cover may cease automatically if class is lost, or the club may decline claims that arise while the ship was out of class. Clubs may also carry out their own condition surveys, often for older ships or on entry. As with hull cover, the club's current rules and the terms of entry are what count.

Cargo insurance and ship classification

Class can matter to cargo insurers too. Many cargo policies include a classification clause that sets standards for the carrying ship. In the London market this is typically the Institute Classification Clause, which in general terms expects the ship to be classed with an IACS member society and to be within certain age limits. Cargo shipped on a ship that does not meet the clause may still be covered, but often only on terms and at a premium agreed in advance. Cargo owners and traders therefore have a reason to check the class of the ship that will carry their goods, not only the shipowner.

How underwriters and brokers can monitor a book of vessels

The obligation to report class changes usually sits with the assured. In practice, insurers and brokers often want their own early warning. The public IACS Vessels in Class dataset makes that possible for ships classed by the 12 IACS members. In the snapshot of 30 September 2026, it lists 60,223 ships, of which 575 are Suspended and 4,848 Withdrawn.

A simple monitoring routine:

  1. Build the list. Export IMO numbers for every insured ship, with the society you expect each to be classed with.
  2. Run a baseline. Store status, class code, last and next survey dates, date of latest status and reason for each ship.
  3. Re-check weekly. Vessel Class Finder refreshes the data every Sunday, so a Monday check picks up the latest changes.
  4. Flag differences. A new Suspended or Withdrawn status, a changed class code, or a next survey date that has passed.
  5. Triage by reason. 'Survey overdue' (1,589 ships in the snapshot), 'Non-compliance with conditions of class / recommendations' (178) and 'Casualty' (67) usually need a call to the assured. 'Transfer of class to another IACS member' (1,316) is often routine but may still need a notification under the policy.
  6. Record and follow up. Ask the assured for the class certificate and survey status, and note the dates in the file.

You can check a single ship with the free lookup, for example IMO 9811000. For a full book, the API accepts up to 100 IMO numbers per request. Our marine insurance use case page covers the fields in detail.

Know the limits. The data covers only IACS-classed ships, it is refreshed weekly rather than live, and it is not a class certificate. It tells you where to ask questions. The policy wording and the society's own records decide the answer.

Frequently asked questions

Is maintaining class a condition of marine insurance?

Typically yes. Hull and machinery policies and P&I club rules usually require the ship to be classed with an approved society and to keep that class. The exact effect depends on the policy wording.

What happens to hull insurance if a ship loses class?

Under many standard wordings cover can terminate automatically when class is suspended, withdrawn or changed, unless insurers agree otherwise. Some wordings defer this until the ship reaches port. Check the classification clause in your policy.

Does P&I cover require class?

P&I club rules typically require the ship to be classed with a society approved by the club and to comply with its recommendations. Loss of class may end cover or affect claims under many club rules.

What is a classification clause in marine insurance?

It is a policy term that requires the ship to be classed with an agreed society and to keep that class. In cargo insurance, a similar clause sets class and age standards for the carrying ship.

How can a broker check whether insured ships are still in class?

Check each IMO number against the IACS Vessels in Class data, store the results and compare them weekly. Look for new suspensions, withdrawals, changes of society and overdue surveys, then confirm with the assured.
