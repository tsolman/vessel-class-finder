What is a transfer of class and why do ships change classification society?

Published 19 October 2026 by WeAreFabbrik. Figures from the IACS Vessels in Class dataset as of 30 September 2026.

A transfer of class is when a ship leaves one classification society and is taken into class by another. Owners usually switch for commercial reasons such as cost, service or a change of owner. Between IACS members, transfers follow an agreed IACS procedure meant to stop owners escaping overdue surveys. A transfer to a non-IACS society is often treated as a risk indicator.

What a transfer of class is

Every classed ship has one classification society that surveys it against its rules. A transfer of class moves that relationship from the losing society to the gaining society. The IMO number stays the same; the society, class notation and survey records change hands.

In the IACS Vessels in Class dataset, the losing society typically records the ship as Withdrawn with a reason such as Transfer of class to another IACS member or Transfer of class to a non-IACS society. If the gaining society is an IACS member, the ship should then appear in its records as in class, usually with the status Delivered. The status codes guide explains each status.

Transfers are common and mostly unremarkable. What matters is where the ship went, what state it was in when it left, and how often it has moved.

Why owners change classification society

Most transfers have ordinary commercial explanations:

  • Change of owner or manager. A buyer often moves newly acquired ships to the society that already classes the rest of its fleet, so one team handles surveys and plan approval.
  • Cost and service. Survey fees, surveyor availability in the ports the ship trades to, and response times all differ between societies.
  • Financing or charter requirements. A lender or charterer may prefer, or require, particular societies.
  • Flag change. Not every society is authorised by every flag state to act on its behalf, so re-flagging can force a change of class.
  • Technical fit. Some societies have more experience with particular ship types or notations.

Some transfers are less benign. An owner facing an expensive survey, a difficult condition of class, or a society that is likely to withdraw class may look for a society that will take the ship with fewer questions. That is the pattern the IACS transfer procedures, and many due diligence checks, are designed to catch.

The IACS Transfer of Class Agreement (TOCA)

IACS members apply a common procedure when a ship moves from one member to another, widely known as the Transfer of Class Agreement, or TOCA. The detail sits in IACS procedural requirements and changes over time, so check the current text on the IACS website before relying on specifics. In general terms, the arrangement is designed so that:

  • The losing society shares the ship's survey status and history with the gaining society, including outstanding conditions of class and recommendations.
  • Overdue surveys and overdue conditions of class are typically dealt with before, or as part of, the transfer, rather than disappearing with the change of society.
  • The gaining society carries out its own survey of the ship as part of accepting it into class, with the scope usually depending on the ship's age and survey position.
  • Outstanding items are carried over, so the ship does not start with a clean sheet it has not earned.

The intent is simple: changing society should not be a way to avoid a survey or a finding. TOCA applies between IACS members. A move to a society outside IACS is not covered by the same shared procedure, which is one reason it gets more attention. See what is IACS and non-IACS classification societies.

Class hopping as a risk pattern

Class hopping is the informal name for moving a ship between societies to avoid the consequences of its condition: an overdue survey, a costly repair, or a looming suspension or withdrawal. It is a pattern, not a recorded status. You infer it from the history.

Signals that commonly prompt a closer look:

  • A transfer to a non-IACS society, especially on an older ship.
  • A transfer that follows closely after a suspension, an overdue survey or a non-compliance finding.
  • More than one change of society in a short period.
  • A transfer at around the same time as a change of name, flag, owner or manager.
  • A ship that leaves IACS class and then disappears from the IACS data altogether.

None of these proves wrongdoing. Plenty of older ships move to smaller societies for cost reasons near the end of their trading life. But in sanctions, KYC and vetting work, these are the patterns most often associated with ships that are harder to inspect, insure or trace. What the data shows

In the IACS snapshot of 30 September 2026 (60,223 ships across the 12 members), the latest recorded status change for 1,747 ships was a transfer of class:

  • 1,316 with the reason Transfer of class to another IACS member.
  • 431 with the reason Transfer of class to a non-IACS society, roughly a quarter of all recorded transfers.

Two cautions. First, these counts only reflect the reason on each record's latest status change; many older transfers will have been overwritten by later events, and a society that records most withdrawals as by society for other reasons will show fewer transfers here. Second, the counts describe the losing society's records and say nothing about the gaining society's standards. Treat the table as a description of the dataset, not a ranking of societies.

What to check when a ship has changed class

  1. Current status. Look the IMO up and confirm the ship is in class now, with which society. The free lookup on Vessel Class Finder shows one ship at a time, for example IMO 9811000; the API checks up to 100 IMOs per request.
  2. The reason and the date. Read reason_for_status with date_of_latest_status. A recent transfer deserves more attention than one ten years ago.
  3. Where it went. If the reason says non-IACS, the ship will not appear in IACS data afterwards. Ask the owner which society classes it now and request the class certificate.
  4. What came before. Ask whether the ship was suspended, had a survey overdue or had open conditions of class at the time of transfer.
  5. Surrounding changes. Compare the transfer date with any change of name, flag, owner or manager from your other sources.
  6. Contract terms. Check whether the new society meets your charter party, policy or loan requirements, which often specify an IACS member.

Class data covers only ships classed by the 12 IACS members and is refreshed weekly. It is one input alongside sanctions screening, ownership research and inspection history, not a replacement for them.

Frequently asked questions

What does transfer of class mean?

It means a ship has moved from one classification society to another. The losing society records class as withdrawn with a transfer reason, and the gaining society takes the ship into its own class.

What is TOCA in shipping?

TOCA is the IACS Transfer of Class Agreement, the common procedure IACS members follow when a ship moves between them. In general terms it requires the survey history and outstanding items to be shared and dealt with, so a transfer cannot be used to escape overdue surveys.

Is a transfer of class a red flag?

A transfer between IACS members is usually routine. A transfer to a non-IACS society, repeated transfers, or a transfer soon after a suspension or overdue survey are commonly treated as reasons to look closer.

What is class hopping?

Class hopping is moving a ship between classification societies to avoid surveys, repairs or a likely loss of class. It is inferred from a ship's class history rather than recorded as a status.

How many ships have transferred to a non-IACS society?

In the 30 September 2026 IACS snapshot, 431 ships carry the reason Transfer of class to a non-IACS society, compared with 1,316 recorded as transferred to another IACS member.

How do I find out which society a ship transferred to?

If it moved to another IACS member, look up the IMO number and the new society will show. If it moved outside IACS, the IACS data will not show the new society, so ask the owner or manager for the current class certificate.
